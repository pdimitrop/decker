The feeling of finally developing your own app is incomparable; especially for us professional developers, who argue on occasion, fail to find common ground and usually either reach the same conclusion from completely unrelated paths, or face an impasse – for people like me, it is refreshing being the master of a project. Don't get me wrong; I learn so much from interacting with peers and I don't intent on giving up. Company ownership over projects and being handed tasks sometimes takes its toll on people. I began experimenting out of pure curiosity, not while searching for a means of escapism.

When I started my first dev job, I'm not ashamed to admit I had little to no developing experience: notably, I hadn't touched Eclipse since 2018; the last Visual Studio edition I had worked on during my studies was VS 2017; Visual Studio Code was my go-to for my university projects; and Notepad++ was my main web development tool – I attempted and failed many times to set up a mock e-shop using Notepad++, Xampp, and whichever database was required by the university at that moment in time. In short, I wasn't up to speed with what .NET devs worked on. Moreover, I was so focused on web development, I hadn't really given much thought to desktop apps. All of that changed once I became employed as a full-stack developer; my skillset skyrocketed thanks to all the hard work I've been putting on and off work all these months. But let's backtrack a bit and start from the beginning.

## Who I am
### Background
Paraskevas I. Dimitropoulos was born on 4 March AD 1999 in Amaroussion, Attica, Hellas. In the same year, a photo was taken of his father sitting in front of his laptop, with his son in front of him, his face fixated on the machine's screen, as if he already knew… His first ever actual computer use came ca. AD 2001, when he was able to visually locate a folder placed on the desktop of the family's Windows 2000 PC, which contained games; his personal favourites were Pipe Dream and Mahjong. At some point the family upgraded to Windows XP and that was when he was introduced to even more software: FreeCell, Hearts, Internet Backgammon, Internet Checkers, Internet Hearts, Internet Reversi, Internet Spades, Minesweeper, Pinball (his personal favourite), Solitaire, and Spider Solitaire. Around that time he was also introduced to "Perris & Katia", the first-ever RAM Kid magazine characters; their first appearance revolved around familiarisation with the computer, while a sequel edition featured lots of fun mini-games. Those close to the family know of a related anecdote: while learning about the parts of the computer's peripherals and UI, he misheard "cursor" (κέρσορας) as "Caesar" (καίσαρας), which combined with Astérix & Obélix films and a Disney children's book on Ancient Rome, led him to confuse "Julius Caesar" (Ιούλιος Καίσαρας) with… "Julius Cursor" (Ιούλιος Κέρσορας)! Growing up, Paraskevas was mostly a console gamer: Gameboy Color, PlayStation 2, PlayStation Portable etc. but he never stopped using the family's PC, strictly off-line, until the age of 8, when he was allowed supervised time to play browser games or watch YouTube videos.
### First steps
Paraskevas always liked puzzles; during his pre-K years he was so bored of his jigsaw puzzles, he would literally turn them upside down and solve them by paying attention to the shapes. After primary school, one thing was certain: while he was genuinely interested in both hardware and software, he struggled with studying maths – not the subject per se, but finding the right way to absorb the information. His first programming-related experience occurred in middle school, where he was taught Logo through a programme developed by the [E-Slate Team](http://e-slate.cti.gr/), loosely translated in English as "Turtleworld". Another loosely related software taught at school was Pivot Animator, a software for step-by-step stick figure 2D animation. Although he was genuinely interested in the logic behind the programme, he never really delved into the world of programming, until ca. AD 2014, when he learned to use Scratch through a webinar. This was a pivotal moment, as from there on, he invested large chunks of his free time in exploring Scratch's capabilities and even building mini-games; at this point in time he was in high school and, encouraged by his IT teacher, almost took part in a national Scratch competition. Ultimately, Paraskevas wasn't satisfied with his work and did not submit his game.
### Programming
While Logo, animation, and Scratch are fun, they are not _really_ programming, but rather an introduction aimed at students. During his second year in high school, the IT teacher used "[GLOSSA](https://alkisg.mysch.gr/)" (ΓΛΩΣΣΑ = language), an open-source pseudo-language used in Hellenic schools. _GLOSSA_ is accompanied by an Interpreter, where users execute simple programmes they write themselves. Actions that can be taken include, but are not limited to: variable declaration, value assignment, loops (e.g. for), et al. The main point of the exercises was the familiarisation with syntax logic, especially reserved terms, as well as the creation of simple, light programmes. There was, however, an extra benefit: preparation for the Panhellenic Exams, which allow students to transition from secondary to tertiary education. You see, there are two major exercises in this particular subject's exams: writing an algorithm, and writing a programme. Different syntaxes, similar logic. Therefore, _GLOSSA_ is an excellent tool for building such an important knowledge base.
